01 / About

A model can be harmless to the public and still fail the person it represents.

AI agents increasingly act on behalf of people. They spend money, negotiate, and take actions that commit their users to binding transactions. An agent can pass its user's data to a counterparty's agent, or accept an inducement that benefits its operator at the user's expense. General AI safety work does not address this problem.

Human institutions manage it through fiduciary duties. Agents that transact for people need technical infrastructure that does the same.

This workshop convenes the communities working on pieces of the problem, who today publish in separate venues: agent safety and evaluation, multi-agent systems, economics and mechanism design, HCI, and technology law. The day ends with breakout groups that draft a public research agenda, which we will publish on this site.

02 / Topics

Topics of interest

We are interested in the technical, economic, and legal infrastructure that keeps AI agents loyal to the people they represent. The call for papers also welcomes related work on privacy, security, and human–agent interaction.

  1. 01

    Permission management

    Authority limits, policy gates, separating proposal from execution, and audit trails that enforce loyalty from outside the model.

  2. 02

    Identity and authenticated delegation

    Agent identity, authorized delegation, and how delegation protocols can carry loyalty guarantees.

  3. 03

    Evaluation and measurement

    Benchmarks for alignment to the user in economic delegation: multi-step transactions, counterparty inducements, and loyalty decay over long horizons.

  4. 04

    Multi-agent delegation

    What happens when one principal's agent deals with another's.

  5. 05

    Transaction and disclosure standards

    Records and disclosures that let principals and counterparties verify what an agent did and on whose behalf.

  6. 06

    Conflicts of interest

    Platform nudges, operator incentives, advertising-funded agents, and undisclosed service degradation, and how to detect and govern them.

  7. 07

    Law and governance

    What fiduciary law, consumer-protection enforcement, and risk frameworks imply for agent design, and which technical properties regulators need agents to have.

  8. 08

    Economic perspectives

    Preference elicitation, market structure for agent intermediaries, and the competition and consumer-protection economics of transacting agents.

05 / Call for papers

Open now

Submit your paper by November 20, 2026.

We welcome three categories: research papers (technical, empirical, or benchmarks), position papers, and law and policy contributions on the topics above.

Submit on OpenReview →

  • Format. Use the AAAI-27 Author Kit. Papers may be up to 8 pages, plus unlimited pages of references. Shorter submissions, including position papers and work in progress, are welcome and will be reviewed relative to their length. Law and policy papers may use footnote-style legal citations within the same template and page limits.
  • Supplementary material. Appendices, code, or data may go after the references or in a separate PDF or ZIP file (up to 50MB). Reviewers are not required to read it.
  • Review. Review is single-blind. Each submission receives at least two reviews from the program committee, with organizers recusing on conflicts.
  • Publication. Accepted papers are non-archival. Authors of original work may opt into a CEUR-WS volume. Accepted papers will be posted on this website unless the authors opt out. Work under review elsewhere is welcome, as is previously published work if the prior venue is disclosed.
  • OpenReview profiles. Every author, including each co-author, needs an OpenReview profile before the paper can be submitted. New profiles without an institutional email can take up to two weeks to be approved, so create them early.

All presentations are in person, so at least one author of each accepted paper must register for AAAI-27 and present in Montréal.
